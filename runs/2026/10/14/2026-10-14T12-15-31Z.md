## Why this is an open problem

Elsholtz and Tao study (f(n)), the number of three-unit-fraction decompositions of (4/n), and describe the universal positivity conjecture: https://arxiv.org/abs/1107.1010 and https://doi.org/10.1017/S1446788712000468 .

Xiaoping Xu's May and September 2026 papers explicitly identify the problem as unresolved, focus on the (p\equiv1\pmod{24}) prime class, and analyze tame and wild families:
- https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23601
- https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29250

Mihnea and Bogdan's 2025 preprint reports finite verification to (10^{18}): https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.00128 . **Our much smaller finite range is a reproducibility and methods test, not a new record or a proof.**

## Exact factorization route

Let (p\equiv1\pmod{24}) be prime, and take (x=(p+3)/4+t) for nonnegative integer (t). Set

[
r=4x-p=3+4t,\qquad A=px.
]

Then (4/p=1/x+1/y+1/z) is equivalent to

[
\frac{r}{A}=\frac1y+\frac1z
\iff (ry-A)(rz-A)=A^2.
]

Thus the search is **complete at a fixed (x)** when it enumerates all positive divisors (d\mid A^2), (d\le A), checks (A+d\equiv A+A^2/d\equiv0\pmod r), and reconstructs

[
y=(A+d)/r,\qquad z=(A+A^2/d)/r.
]

All equalities and divisibility checks use arbitrary-precision **integers**; no floating-point arithmetic determines a certificate. The code checks the identity (4xyz=p(xy+xz+yz)) for each recorded witness.

### Special first-denominator lemma (elementary; originality not claimed)

For (t=0), (r=3), (x=(p+3)/4\equiv1\pmod3), and (A\equiv1\pmod3). A solution with **this specific (x)** exists **if and only if** (x) has at least one prime factor congruent to (2\pmod3).

- Necessity: (d=3y-A\mid A^2) must be (2\pmod3). Since the prime (p\equiv1\pmod3), one of the prime factors of (x) must be (2\pmod3).
- Sufficiency: if (q\mid x) and (q\equiv2\pmod3), choose (d=q). Then (A+d\equiv0\pmod3) and, because (A^2\equiv1\pmod3), (A^2/q\equiv2\pmod3); both reconstructed denominators are integers and positive.

This only characterizes the **first (x)** and does **not** resolve other values of (x) or the universal conjecture.

## What failed or remains unproved

1. Searching (x=(p+3)/4) alone leaves **35,750** eligible primes uncovered. The special-case lemma explains why the first choice fails in those cases.
2. An offset bound (t\le26) observed for these 82,887 primes is **not** a proven bound for all primes.
3. This verification is far smaller than published preprint bounds; its value is a small transparent algorithm and precise certificates, **not** an unprecedented computation.
4. We did **not** independently recheck all published general prior art or formally verify the implementation in a theorem prover. Independent brute comparison was performed only on the 14 small primes stated above.
5. A full resolution would require a proof for every (n\ge2), or an independently established counterexample—not a pattern extrapolated from finite data.
